A forklift charger has to match the battery on four points: voltage, amp output, input power, and connector. Get those right and almost any modern charger will do the job; get one wrong and the charger either will not connect, will not finish a cycle in your shift window, or will shorten the battery's life. Here is how to read each one off the equipment you already have.

## 1. Voltage: read the battery tag

Forklift batteries are 24V, 36V, 48V, or 80V. The voltage is printed on the battery's data tag, and it also follows from the cell count: lead-acid cells are 2 V each, so 12 cells is 24V, 18 cells is 36V, 24 cells is 48V, and 40 cells is 80V. The charger must be the same nominal voltage. There is no "close enough."

Common pairings: walkie pallet jacks and small stackers run 24V; three-wheel and narrow-aisle trucks run 36V or 48V; sit-down counterbalance trucks run 36V, 48V, or 80V depending on size and manufacturer.

## 2. Amps: size from the battery's amp-hour rating

The battery tag also lists capacity in amp-hours (Ah), usually at the 6-hour rate. Charger output is chosen so the battery recharges fully in the time you have.

A charger that is too small never finishes the cycle, and a lead-acid battery that is chronically undercharged sulfates. A charger that is much too large heats the battery; high-frequency chargers manage this with temperature compensation, older ferroresonant units do not. If you run one shift and charge overnight, the 16–18% column is the right answer and the cheapest charger.

## 3. Input power: what the wall can supply

Chargers up to roughly 100 A output are commonly available in single-phase 208–240 V. Above that, most chargers require three-phase 208, 240, or 480 V. Before ordering, confirm what the panel near your charging area actually has; adding a three-phase circuit is often the most expensive part of the installation. Many high-frequency chargers accept a wide input range and auto-detect, which simplifies the choice if you move equipment between buildings.

## 4. Connector

The charger plug must match the battery connector: Anderson SB175 or SB350 (grey or red housings, keyed by voltage color), Euro DIN 160/320, or the manufacturer's own. The connector color and pin size are a safety interlock against plugging a 48V charger into a 36V battery, so do not swap housings to make a mismatched pair fit. If the truck has an on-board charger, the input is a standard wall cord and this step does not apply.

## Conventional vs high-frequency

**Ferroresonant and SCR chargers** are the older technology: heavy, simple, tolerant of abuse, and around 80–85% efficient. They run a fixed curve and do not adjust for battery temperature or age.

**High-frequency (HF) chargers** use switching electronics, weigh a fraction as much, run 90–95% efficient, and read the battery to adjust the charge curve. They also support multiple chemistries (flooded, AGM, gel, lithium) on the same unit. For a new purchase, HF is the default unless you specifically need a drop-in replacement for an existing ferroresonant setup.

## On-board vs off-board

On-board chargers ride on the truck and plug into a wall outlet; they are standard on walkies and small stackers and are limited to lower output because of size and heat. Off-board chargers sit at a charging station and handle anything from 24V walkie batteries to 80V counterbalance batteries at high current. If you run more than one shift or swap batteries, off-board is the only practical option.

## Lithium

Lithium (LiFePO4) forklift batteries need a charger with a lithium profile: a constant-current/constant-voltage curve that stops at the BMS's voltage limit with no equalization stage. Most HF chargers offer a lithium setting; a lead-acid-only charger will either undercharge the pack or trip its BMS. Lithium also tolerates opportunity charging without the sulfation penalty lead-acid suffers, which is the main operational reason fleets convert.

## What to have in hand before you order

1. Battery voltage and Ah from the tag
2. Number of shifts and the longest window the battery sits idle
3. Input voltage and phase available at the charger location
4. Connector type and color on the battery
5. Chemistry (flooded, AGM/gel, lithium)

With those five, the charger is a lookup rather than a guess.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use a 36V charger on a 48V forklift battery?**
No. Charger and battery voltage must match. The keyed connector is designed to prevent this, and forcing it can damage the battery and the charger.

**How long does a forklift battery take to charge?**
With a correctly sized conventional charger, about 8 hours plus a cool-down period before use. HF fast chargers can complete a cycle in 4–6 hours; opportunity charging keeps the battery topped up in shorter bursts without a full cycle.

**Is a bigger charger always better?**
No. Above about 25–30% of the battery's Ah rating, a lead-acid battery heats up and its life shortens unless the charger has temperature compensation. Size for the window you have, not the fastest possible charge.

**What does "opportunity charging" mean?**
Charging during breaks and shift changes instead of a single full cycle. It works well with lithium and with HF chargers designed for it; done with a conventional charger on a flooded battery, it causes sulfation.

**Single-phase or three-phase?**
Single-phase 208–240 V covers chargers up to roughly 100 A output. Above that you generally need three-phase. Check the panel before you buy.
