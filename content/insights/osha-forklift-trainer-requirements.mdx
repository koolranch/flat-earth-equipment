OSHA does not certify forklift trainers, and it does not require a train-the-trainer course. The entire federal requirement for who may train and evaluate operators is one sentence in [29 CFR 1910.178(l)(2)(iii)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178): training and evaluation must be conducted by "persons who have the knowledge, training, and experience to train powered industrial truck operators and evaluate their competence."

The employer decides who meets that bar, and the employer carries the liability if the choice was unreasonable. Everything below is about making that choice defensible.

## What "knowledge, training, and experience" means in practice

OSHA has explained the phrase in interpretation letters and in inspections. A trainer should be able to show all three:

- **Knowledge** of the standard itself (1910.178), the training topics in paragraph (l)(3), and the specific hazards of your workplace.
- **Training** in how to operate the trucks in question. That can be a formal course, a prior employer's program, or documented in-house training. It should be on the same class of truck the trainer will teach.
- **Experience** operating those trucks in a real working environment. Time in the seat matters because the evaluator has to recognize unsafe technique, not just recite rules.

A supervisor who has run counterbalance trucks for ten years and has read the standard qualifies. A safety coordinator who has never driven a forklift does not, even with a train-the-trainer certificate, because they cannot credibly evaluate operator competence.

## Do you need a train-the-trainer certificate?

No. Third-party train-the-trainer courses are useful for structuring a program and for giving the trainer documented training, but the certificate itself is not an OSHA requirement and does not by itself qualify anyone. OSHA inspectors ask how the trainer was qualified; a certificate is one piece of evidence, operating experience is another, and a written designation from the employer is a third.

## Can the trainer be an employee?

Yes, and at most sites they should be. In-house trainers know the trucks, the aisles, the dock, and the loads. The standard is written around site-specific training, so an outside instructor who has never seen your building still needs your supervisor to handle the workplace-specific portion and the evaluation.

Contractors and consultants can deliver the formal-instruction piece and can perform evaluations if they meet the same knowledge, training, and experience test.

## What the trainer is responsible for

Under 1910.178(l), the trainer or evaluator handles:

1. **Formal instruction** on the truck-related and workplace-related topics listed in (l)(3): controls, capacity, stability, inspection, refueling or charging, surface conditions, pedestrian traffic, ramps, and the loads handled.
2. **Practical training**: demonstrations by the trainer and supervised exercises by the trainee.
3. **Evaluation** of the operator performing actual work on the truck they will use.
4. **Refresher training** when an operator is seen operating unsafely, has an accident or near miss, fails an evaluation, is assigned a different truck type, or when workplace conditions change.
5. **The three-year evaluation** of every operator.

## What the record has to show

The employer's certification record must include the operator's name, the date of training, the date of evaluation, and the identity of the person who performed the training or evaluation. Keep a short trainer qualification file alongside it: how the trainer was trained, on which truck classes, and how long they have operated them. When OSHA asks "who trained this operator and why were they qualified," that file is the answer.

## Running a hybrid program

Most employers now split the work. Operators complete the formal-instruction portion online, then the in-house trainer runs hands-on practice and the evaluation. That keeps the classroom content consistent across shifts and sites, and it puts the part that legally requires judgment—watching the operator run the truck—in the hands of someone who knows the equipment.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does OSHA have an approved list of forklift trainers?**
No. There is no OSHA registry, license, or approval for trainers. Any organization claiming "OSHA-approved trainer" status is describing its own program, not an OSHA credential.

**Can a forklift operator train other operators?**
Yes, if they have the knowledge of the standard and the experience to evaluate competence. Many sites designate lead operators as trainers and give them a copy of the standard and a short course on how to run an evaluation.

**Does the trainer have to be certified on every truck class?**
The trainer needs training and experience on the type of truck they are teaching. Someone qualified on sit-down counterbalance trucks should not evaluate a reach-truck operator without their own reach-truck experience.

**How often does a trainer need refresher training?**
The standard sets no interval for trainers. Trainers who also operate are subject to the same three-year evaluation as any operator, and it is good practice to have them review the standard and your written program annually.

**What happens if OSHA decides the trainer was not qualified?**
The operators' certifications are treated as invalid, which turns one questionable trainer into a citation for every operator they trained. This is why documenting the trainer's qualification matters as much as documenting the operator's.
